A nine-phase silicon program

The generic lifecycle unpacked one level, the way a real silicon program runs it: hardware and software/firmware as two parallel tracks against the same nine phases, with one planned gate at the end of each phase. These are the phases and gates of the shipped silicon profile.

  1. HW1. Concept
    SW/FWEarly SW architecture
    G1 Concept Approval
  2. HW2. Requirements
    SW/FWSW requirements and security
    G2 Requirements Freeze
  3. HW3. Planning
    SW/FWDev plan, CI/CD
    G3 Plan of Record Baseline
  4. HW4. Design
    SW/FWVirtual platform, FPGA dev
    G4 Design Freeze
  5. HW5. Verification & Sign-off
    SW/FWPre-silicon validation
    G5 Tape-Out Readiness Review
  6. HW6. Tape Out
    SW/FWFW and driver code freeze
    G6 Tape-Out Sign-off
  7. HW7. Bring-Up
    SW/FWFW and BMC bring-up
    G7 Power-On Success
  8. HW8. Testing (post-silicon validation)
    SW/FWPlatform enablement
    G8 Qualification Exit
  9. HW9. Production Release
    SW/FWProduction FW, OTA updates
    G9 Production Release

Both tracks hard-stop together exactly once, at Tape Out. Hardware's stop is absolute: the layout is released to the foundry and no further RTL or layout change is physically possible. Firmware freezes in lockstep, because that image is what goes onto first silicon. Everywhere else software and firmware keep developing against the frozen spec while hardware moves through fab, and hardware often starts early feasibility work during Planning. The tracks converge again at Bring-Up, the first time firmware runs on real silicon.

The program lifecycle and its gates

The eight-phase spine everything else hangs off (PLC is Program Life Cycle, not Product Life Cycle). The diamonds are the seven planned gates: calendar events the customer signs off on in the SOW, never surprises. Profiles may be finer-grained; the shipped silicon profile has nine gates (view 2).

  2. 2. Award / ContractSOW signed, scope locked, kickoff

  4. 3. Design / BuildRTL design + verification
  5. Requirements Freeze
  6. Design Freeze

Planned gates are calendar events. Issue-triggered gates (not shown) are the opposite: unscheduled, forced by a Sev 1 out of the issue flow. Mixing the two in status reporting is how executives lose the ability to tell "on plan" from "in crisis".

Program health metrics

"Status: Amber" means nothing without a rule behind it. Five metrics, each with defined green, amber and red limits, make a status defensible instead of a gut call. A metric with too little data is shown as not rated, never as green.

Schedule Variance

Forecast or actual date of the last planned gate, minus its baseline date, as a share of the baseline duration

  • Green 3% behind or less (or ahead)
  • Amber up to 8% behind
  • Red more than 8% behind, or any open gate past its planned date

Gate On-Time Rate

Completed gates passed on or before baseline, over completed gates

  • Green 90% or more
  • Amber 75% to 89%
  • Red under 75%

Issue Aging

Days each open Sev 1 and Sev 2 issue has been open, against its limits

  • Green Sev 1 up to 7 days, Sev 2 up to 14
  • Amber Sev 1 up to 14, Sev 2 up to 28
  • Red Sev 1 over 14, Sev 2 over 28

Escape Rate

Issues found later than the gate that should have caught them, over all issues, per phase

  • Green under 10%
  • Amber 10% to 20%
  • Red over 20%

Budget Burn Ratio

Actual spend over planned spend, to date

  • Green 0.9 to 1.1
  • Amber 1.1 to 1.25, or below 0.9
  • Red over 1.25, or forecast over budget

Program status = the worst of the five, never an average

One red metric makes the whole program red whatever the other four say. Averaging is how a program hides one bad number behind four good ones.

Sources: Module 08 and the metric definitions. The numbers above come from the silicon profile; pmf health computes them and pmf dashboard draws them.
